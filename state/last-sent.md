*🟡 AI usage rules draft updated at IETF*
_IETF — AI Preferences working group · November 17, 2026_

*Synopsis*
The IETF quietly edited its AI Preferences working group page. Nothing was announced.
· A draft titled "Machine-Readable Training Preference Signal" moved to version 01. It is dated November 16, 2026, and replaces the October 30 version.
· The page also lists drafts on attribution and rights marks, verifiable compliance records, and a verifiable-credential binding.
· These are early drafts, not finished standards.

*What the terms mean*
IETF — the body that writes the technical standards the internet runs on
Working group — a team inside the IETF that drafts one standard
Internet-Draft — an early public proposal that anyone can comment on
AI usage preferences — a site's stated rules for how AI may use its content
Training — using your content to teach an AI model

*Why it matters for your content*
A standard way to say "don't train on this, but do cite it" could replace today's patchwork of site-by-site settings. It is still early. But the rules that come out of here will decide how much control you have over AI use of your pages.

*Example*
A publisher wants its guides cited in AI answers but not used to train models. Today it needs separate settings per AI company. A shared signal could let it say this once, in one place.

*Do this*
Nothing to do yet — just know it's coming.

*🟡 Analytics tools disagree wildly on traffic estimates*
_arXiv — AI search & citation research · November 17, 2026_

*Synopsis*
An academic paper measured how reliable SEO and AI-answer tools are, using a large US telecom provider's website as the test case.
· Two commercial analytics platforms differed by 7.5x to 27.1x on monthly visits for one site section.
· They did agree on which section was bigger in 60 of 66 pairs.
· Review demand and search volume agreed partly on broad branches (0.47 to 0.75) and barely on topics (0.18).
· Of four predictions about AI answers citing big companies, one held, one held in direction only, and two failed.
· Video and community sites took a median 21% of citations on Google's AI Overviews.

*What the terms mean*
SEO — making your pages easy for search engines to find and rank
GEO/AEO — the same idea for AI answers, so they mention and cite you
Search volume — how many people search a phrase each month
Citation — a link an AI answer shows as its source
AI Overviews — the AI-written answer Google puts above the blue links

*Why it matters for your content*
Tool numbers are estimates, not facts. They are better at ranking than at sizing. Search volume also misses demand that shows up in places like customer reviews. Budget and priority calls built on one tool's raw numbers can be badly off.

*Example*
Your team plans a page for a topic one tool says gets 50,000 searches a month. A second tool says 2,000. Use both to rank topics. Then confirm with your own customer questions and reviews.

*Do this*
Treat tool traffic numbers as rough ranges, and cross-check priority topics against customer reviews and support questions.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19655

*🟡 AI answers about brands mostly cite other sites*
_arXiv — AI search & citation research · November 17, 2026_

*Synopsis*
An audit of 167,551 citations in archived AI answers about 128 brands, across 13 languages and 12 home markets.
· In the main Nordic-Baltic set, 3,863 of 21,033 sites account for 80% of 131,514 citations.
· Wikipedia leads in 11 of 12 query languages.
· 82.4% of citations point outside sites tied to the brand. 17.6% point to sites tied to the brand.
· In the Polish data, four recruitment sites got 637 citations. Polish Wikipedia got 297.
· The study shows displayed links only. It does not show what causes them.

*What the terms mean*
Nothing new to define.

*Why it matters for your content*
Most of what AI cites about your brand sits on sites you don't own. Reviews, encyclopedias and job boards shape the answer as much as your website does. A small group of sites gets most of the citations, so being accurate and present on those matters most.

*Example*
Someone asks an AI assistant, "Is Brand X a good employer?" The answer cites a job-review site and Wikipedia. Your careers page is not cited. Fixing the facts on those two sources changes the answer more than rewriting your careers page.

*Do this*
List the top 10 sites cited when AI is asked about your brand, and check each for outdated or wrong facts.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25787

*⚪ Google AI Mode adds private temporary threads*
_Google — The Keyword (Search) · November 17, 2026_

*Synopsis*
Google announced temporary threads in AI Mode.
· Sessions are detached from the user's account.
· Answers are not shaped by past preferences. They don't affect future personalization and aren't saved to history.
· Closing the thread deletes it.
· It is rolling out globally on desktop and mobile browsers, in all languages AI Mode supports.

*What the terms mean*
AI Mode — Google's chat-style search experience, where you ask follow-up questions
Personalization — answers tailored to a user's past searches and preferences

*Why it matters for your content*
Some AI Mode users will now get answers with no personal history behind them. Those answers rest on the query and the content alone. So a clear, well-sourced page matters more than a loyal audience in those sessions.

*Example*
A shopper opens a temporary thread to ask, "best running shoes for flat feet." There are no past clicks to lean on. The page that answers that exact question clearly is the one that can be cited.

*Do this*
Test your key queries in a temporary thread to see AI Mode without your own history.

https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/temporary-threads-ai-mode/

*⚪ New attack can steer AI research reports*
_arXiv — AI search & citation research · November 17, 2026_

*Synopsis*
A survey paper on the security of AI research tools. It also proposes a new attack and tests it.
· It coded 34 earlier studies. Only 26.5% looked at the planning stage, and none tested poisoning end to end on commercial products.
· In 3,520 closed test runs, GEO tricks and prompt injection were nearly ineffective.
· The new "agenda seeding" attack made the attacker's target the top pick in 16% to 28% of reports.
· The attacker writes no instructions. They only introduce a made-up evaluation metric.
· The proposed defense, provenance tagging, keeps usefulness but can be bypassed by multi-site bait.

*What the terms mean*
Deep research agents — AI tools that search the web on their own and write a cited report
Prompt injection — hiding instructions in a page to make an AI obey them
Agenda seeding — planting a made-up scoring metric so the AI ranks the attacker's product first
Provenance tagging — labelling where each piece of evidence came from

*Why it matters for your content*
Hidden-instruction tricks did not work in these tests. Framing the comparison did. This is an attack, not a tactic to copy. But a competitor could use it against you. Reports from AI research tools may rank you on a metric nobody else uses.

*Example*
An AI research report ranks CRM tools by "onboarding velocity score." You have never heard of that metric. One rival ranks first on it, and the metric traces back to a few pages from that rival.

*Do this*
Don't copy it. Check whether AI research reports in your category rank brands on metrics you don't recognize.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20045

*⚪ AI invents facts about rarely cited sources*
_arXiv — AI search & citation research · November 19, 2026_

*Synopsis*
A study of 1.92 million academic papers across eight fields, tested on three leading AI models. It asked who wrote each paper.
· Hallucination rates topped 98% for rarely cited papers and fell below 37% for highly cited ones.
· The pattern held across all three models and all eight fields.
· The failures mostly reflect trouble retrieving what the model knows, not missing knowledge.

*What the terms mean*
Hallucination — an AI stating something false with confidence

*Why it matters for your content*
The less a fact appears across the web, the more likely AI gets it wrong. The study tested papers, not brands. But the same pattern would hit small brands, niche products and lesser-known people. Widely repeated, consistent facts are safer.

*Example*
Your flagship product gets described correctly. Your founder's name or a smaller product line gets mixed up with a competitor's. Few pages state those facts, so the AI fills the gap.

*Do this*
Ask AI assistants five factual questions about your lesser-known products and people, and note what they get wrong.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23530
