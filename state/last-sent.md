*🟠 AI agents now judge your site, not just your buzz*
_arXiv — AI search & citation research · September 28, 2026_

*What happened*
A study tracked 37,927 AI agent journeys across four AI platforms and 1,056 real businesses. Only 7-10% of a finished AI answer comes from the model's training knowledge — the rest comes from web pages the agent reads live. Businesses that are easy for agents to fetch and read got recommended 1.9x more often, with answers 41% more accurate.

*What the terms mean*
Agent experience (AX) — how easily an AI agent can open and read your actual website, not just find you mentioned elsewhere.
Agent journey — one AI's multi-step research process: it runs several searches and opens several pages before giving one answer.

*Why it matters for your content*
Being cited or mentioned somewhere isn't enough anymore. If an AI agent can't fetch and read your own pages, a competitor who's easier to read gets recommended instead — even with equal buzz.

*Example*
Someone asks an AI assistant for "best project management tool for small agencies." The AI opens your pricing and comparison pages directly. If those pages are blocked, slow, or hard to parse, it answers from a review site instead — and you're left out of the recommendation.

*Do this*
Check whether AI crawlers can actually fetch and read your key pages — pricing, comparisons, product pages — not just whether you're mentioned elsewhere.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34951v1

*🟠 Cloudflare separates AI training access from search access*
_Cloudflare Blog (AI) · September 15, 2026_

*What happened*
Cloudflare published guidance on "mixed-use" AI crawlers — bots that both power search results and feed AI training. It explains how site owners can allow the search-related use while blocking the training use.

*What the terms mean*
Mixed-use crawler — a single bot that both indexes your pages for search and feeds their text into AI model training, so blocking it outright can also hurt your search visibility.

*Why it matters for your content*
Until now, blocking a crawler from training your content risked also blocking it from search. This gives more precise control: stay visible in search, opt out of training.

*Example*
A publisher currently blocks a crawler entirely in robots.txt to stop AI training, which also cuts off any search-linked use of that same bot. The new approach lets them keep the search-related access while opting out only of training.

*Do this*
Review your robots.txt and crawler rules to see if you can allow search-related crawling while blocking AI training specifically.

https://blog.cloudflare.com/accountable-mixed-use-ai-crawlers/

*🟡 Old SEO checklist doesn't predict AI citations*
_arXiv — AI search & citation research · September 28, 2026_

*What happened*
A study analyzed about 2 million AI citations from ChatGPT, Claude, Google AI, and Gemini, across 10,000 pages from B2B SaaS companies. The strongest predictor of being cited was how closely a page's wording matched real buyer questions — not FAQ blocks, structured data, or Core Web Vitals. Those classic checklist items showed no real effect once other factors were controlled for. Overall domain authority mattered six times more than any single on-page tweak.

*What the terms mean*
Structured data — hidden code tags that describe page content to search engines (e.g. marking up a price or rating).
Core Web Vitals — Google's page-speed and load-quality scores.

*Why it matters for your content*
Checklist tactics like FAQ schema or speed fixes won't move AI citations on their own. What matters more is whether your page's wording matches how buyers actually phrase their questions, and whether your whole domain is seen as authoritative.

*Example*
Adding an FAQ schema block to a landing page looks like an AEO win, but if the page says "solution for X" while buyers search "best tool for X," it still won't get cited more.

*Do this*
Audit your page wording against real buyer phrasing, not just checklist boxes like schema and speed.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35077v1
